# CPT-justification-of-contract-law

## What it means

The question why the institution of contract law exists, whether it is meaningful and what difference it makes — including welfare-economic, promissory-moral, sociological and other accounts of what contracting practice accomplishes. A candidate concept coined during the ingest of SRC-0025 and drawn from its claims [CLM-0025-004] [CLM-0025-013] [CLM-0025-014] [CLM-0025-015] [CLM-0025-017].

## Claims

### Descriptive

**general**

- There is a dearth of empirical evidence whether the legal institution of contract law makes any difference to investment, morality, or a better life, and the quantitative and qualitative research that does exist is at best unresolved on that question. — rests on literature (SRC-0025). [CLM-0025-013]
- Established scholarly accounts of why people engage in contracting practices fall into three categories: contracts are very meaningful because they enhance economic welfare (lawyers and economists starting from rational-actor axioms or from bounded rationality); contracts are very meaningful because they embody in law the moral principle that people should honor their promises; and contracts are not nearly as meaningful as thought (work at the intersection of law and sociology). — rests on literature (SRC-0025). [CLM-0025-014]

### Interpretative

**general**

- The practice of making contracts has less to do with the logic of the text than it does with ritual, self-actualization, and the unrequited human desire for certainty; contracts are rituals that help avoid violence as a means of conflict resolution and serve higher orders of self-fulfillment. — rests on abstract considerations (SRC-0025). [CLM-0025-004]
- A fourth, contrarian account of contracting: contracts are meaningful because people think they are and rely on them like monkey-ropes, even if that reliance is something of a delusion — contracts, rather than being very meaningful or not nearly as meaningful as one thought, are means of groping for order, reassurance, fulfillment, and certainty in the face of chaos, insecurity, and the unknown unknowns; they can be delusions of order. — rests on abstract considerations (SRC-0025). [CLM-0025-015]
- The idea that the logic of a 'science' of contract law can explain all transacting behavior is delusional — as delusional as a physicist insisting on baryonic matter as the end-all of cosmology; contract practice, in contrast to the economists' delusion of a 'complete contract', incorporates all kinds of apparently irrational practices based on beliefs that may well be illusions or delusions. — rests on abstract considerations (SRC-0025). [CLM-0025-017]
