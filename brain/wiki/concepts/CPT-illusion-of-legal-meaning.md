# CPT-illusion-of-legal-meaning

## What it means

The appearance of legal or semantic meaningfulness produced by replicating the form of law or language without its substance, which users mistake for actual meaning — as in pseudolegal rituals or large language model outputs. An emergent concept coined during the ingest of SRC-0006 and drawn from its claims [CLM-0006-001] [CLM-0006-002] [CLM-0006-010] [CLM-0006-013] [CLM-0006-014] [CLM-0006-015] [CLM-0006-016] [CLM-0006-017]. Promoted from candidate to emergent at this close-out: claims from 4 sources with no shared author are mapped to it (SRC-0006, SRC-0010, SRC-0024, SRC-0025).

## Claims

### Descriptive

**US**

- Some of what lawyers, both academic and practicing, believe about contract law emanates from illusions and delusions embedded in and around the doctrinal canon — phrases like 'the meeting of the minds' or 'the document speaks for itself' — which are not easily dismissed as mere metaphors and persist because it is difficult to demarcate myth from history or belief from knowledge. — rests on abstract considerations (SRC-0025). [CLM-0025-001]

**general**

- Large language models make predictions about the most likely next word in a sequence by treating each word as a number ('words-as-vectors'); they make no attempt to understand or externally validate the meaning of the terms used — all that matters is statistical closeness — so their outputs have the appearance of meaning while there is no external verification of meaning and no underlying intentionality in the use of words. — rests on literature (SRC-0006). [CLM-0006-013]
- Even without legal fine-tuning, commercially available large language models can generate legal-like texts hardly distinguishable from human-created legal texts, although their outputs are overly generic and heavily dependent on the abilities of the human prompter. — rests on factual basis (SRC-0010). [CLM-0010-006]
- Because large language models predict how words will be used in context based on past usage, they are particularly ill-suited to weigh and balance competing values and interests in making policy decisions in the same manner as an agency or a judge; they will implicitly make policy decisions without disclosing them, present their conclusions with an air of objectivity, and are likely to be particularly untrustworthy in evaluating the lawfulness of rules where there is no precedent on the scope of statutory authority. — rests on abstract considerations (SRC-0024). [CLM-0024-014]
- Even theorists whose work justifies a text-focused approach to contract interpretation, such as Alan Schwartz and Robert Scott, and the proponents of LLM-based generative interpretation pay lip service to the 'mutual intention' or 'shared intention of the parties' trope even as their own theses undercut it. — rests on literature (SRC-0025). [CLM-0025-005]

### Interpretative

**AU**

- Because the law uses archaic language and often Byzantine ritual and is conceptually inaccessible to the general member of the public, it is far too easy for an alienated and excluded layperson to mistake pseudolegal behaviour that mimics the form of law for law itself; excluding the population from meaningful engagement with the law robs them of the ability to distinguish between law and not-law. — rests on abstract considerations (SRC-0006). [CLM-0006-010]

**US**

- 'Mutual intention' and its corresponding metaphor 'the meeting of the minds' are metaphysical nonsense: there is no such thing as a mutual intention of the parties in a colorable contract interpretation dispute, and no shared mind or 'shared intention' thought bubble that can be known rather than posited by belief. — rests on abstract considerations (SRC-0025). [CLM-0025-002]
- The third party who observes and understands the objective manifestations of contractual agreement can be a large language model, even though it is a proxy for a human mind and does not think like one; this belies the need for the shared-intention illusion, and since 'shared intention' can never be known, the result will be more satisfying as a rational result than a mere coin flip or roll of the dice. — rests on abstract considerations (SRC-0025). [CLM-0025-008]

**general**

- Pseudolaw involves, at its heart, a mistaking of the form of legal argumentation for its substance: through the use of legalistic language, archaic sources and strange rituals, an illusion of legal meaning is created which tricks users into believing their actions are legally meaningful. — rests on abstract considerations (SRC-0006). [CLM-0006-001]
- Pseudolaw shares common features with generative AI: both produce an illusion of meaning which users mistake for actual meaning, both succeed in replicating the form of their mimicked output but not its substance, and both flourish where their users desperately wish to believe in the efficacy of the output. — rests on abstract considerations (SRC-0006). [CLM-0006-002]
- Users of pseudolaw generally hold a genuine belief that what they are doing represents actual law because they are experiencing a kind of pareidolia — the human tendency to perceive meaningful patterns in unrelated stimuli: the ritualistic words appear so like how they expect law to look that they think they are looking at law. — rests on abstract considerations (SRC-0006). [CLM-0006-014]
- Users of pseudolaw are notably more confident in the presentation of their arguments than other litigants in person; this may be a consequence of users mistaking confidence for substantive knowledge, drawing on the heuristic of treating confidence as a proxy for knowledge (the 'confidence heuristic'): the very confidence with which pseudolegal materials mimic legal forms makes adherents more likely to believe that what is presented is 'true law'. — rests on abstract considerations (SRC-0006). [CLM-0006-015]
- Pseudolaw and large language models can both be highly successful in a certain way, provided the relevant domain focuses on form rather than substance; for pseudolaw, this form-as-success helps explain both the communicative expertise developed by some users and the role of gurus who sell access to the specialised knowledge and language forms they purport to possess — a success of form and not substance. — rests on abstract considerations (SRC-0006). [CLM-0006-016]
- Pseudolaw appears to offer something deeply desirable — genuine, empowering access to specialised knowledge achieved through special ritualised forms; magical thinking is at the heart of most sovereign citizen pseudolaw, and recognition of the appeal of this magical thinking by users is vital if judges and educators are to help users abandon the practice. — rests on literature (SRC-0006). [CLM-0006-017]
- A fourth, contrarian account of contracting: contracts are meaningful because people think they are and rely on them like monkey-ropes, even if that reliance is something of a delusion — contracts, rather than being very meaningful or not nearly as meaningful as one thought, are means of groping for order, reassurance, fulfillment, and certainty in the face of chaos, insecurity, and the unknown unknowns; they can be delusions of order. — rests on abstract considerations (SRC-0025). [CLM-0025-015]
- The idea that the logic of a 'science' of contract law can explain all transacting behavior is delusional — as delusional as a physicist insisting on baryonic matter as the end-all of cosmology; contract practice, in contrast to the economists' delusion of a 'complete contract', incorporates all kinds of apparently irrational practices based on beliefs that may well be illusions or delusions. — rests on abstract considerations (SRC-0025). [CLM-0025-017]

### Prescriptive

**general**

- The sooner the illusions of provable mutual intentions are discarded and the delusions that contracts are anything other than imperfect maps on a highly complex reality put aside, the better off contracting parties will be; it takes a village to interpret the signs people make to each other, because there are no private languages and the words and phrases are borrowed from the community in an attempt to be understood by way of objective manifestations. — rests on abstract considerations (SRC-0025). [CLM-0025-018]

## Open questions

- Can systems that make no attempt to validate meaning be entrusted with tasks where interpretation is essential?
- Can text produced without understood or validated meaning acquire the function of law through use alone?
- May an adjudicator delegate the observer's role in interpretation to a model without violating the duty to decide?
- Are interpretive determinations inherently normative tasks that models should not perform?
