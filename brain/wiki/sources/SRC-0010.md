# SRC-0010 — The Death of the Legal Author: Authority, Intention, and Law-Creation in the Advent of GenAI

## What the paper argues

The paper takes up a question it holds to be largely unattended in the philosophical debate on generative AI and law — not whether AI should create and interpret law, but whether it can, and whether AI can be a legal author [CLM-0010-001]. It reconstructs the view in contemporary legal theory that an intention-bearing author is postulated as the condition of the possibility of law-creation [CLM-0010-002] and that an intention behind the law is the condition of the possibility of law-interpretation [CLM-0010-003], distinguishing three kinds of intention relevant to law-creation — type, token and causal — no one of which necessarily entails the others [CLM-0010-004]. It then holds that systematic testing confirms LLMs produce convincingly legal outputs displaying all signs of what is usually called legal reasoning [CLM-0010-005], that even without legal fine-tuning they generate legal-like texts hardly distinguishable from human-created ones [CLM-0010-006], and that real-life legislators have already used ChatGPT to draft bills, at least one of which passed into a legal system [CLM-0010-007]. Yet LLMs generate these texts without any intention, possessing no intention-constituting mental states [CLM-0010-008], while still being agents whose outputs compare with those of a human law-creator [CLM-0010-009]; no technical limitation has yet been found showing them in principle unable to perform law-creation and law-interpretation tasks [CLM-0010-010]. On the dominant intentionalist view this would mean ChatGPT outputs cannot be law and their reading cannot be interpretation [CLM-0010-011], and even a human-prompted generation lacks the token intention on which interpretation of the actual author's intention would rest [CLM-0010-012]. The paper sides instead with positions that dispense with intention: artefacts without authorial intention are conceivable when use maintains their proper function, so AI outputs can be legal artefacts when used as law [CLM-0010-013]; recipients are fully equipped to determine a text's use functions, authority and normative content without any necessary intention [CLM-0010-014]; and so LLM outputs can count as law on the condition that their function is legal [CLM-0010-015].

## Claims

### Descriptive

- In the philosophical debate on generative artificial intelligence and law, most attention has gone to the normative question whether AI should create and interpret law, while the prior question whether AI can create and interpret law — whether AI is capable of producing outputs that can be deemed law, and whether AI can be a legal author — has remained largely unattended. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0010-001]
- In contemporary legal theory and philosophy, the possibility of an agent having a mental state called intention is often postulated as the condition of the possibility of law-creation: intention to legislate is constitutive of the law-creating act, in the sense that law cannot be created absent an intention on the part of the author. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0010-002]
- In legal theory, the existence of an intention behind the law is widely treated as the condition of the possibility of law-interpretation: on intentionalist accounts, interpretation is impossible without intention, and there is no meaning in the lack of an author or of an intended meaning. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0010-003]
- Systematic testing of large language models on human and machine benchmarks confirms their practical usefulness for lawyers: large language models can produce outputs that are convincingly legal and display all signs of what is usually called legal reasoning. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0010-005]
- Even without legal fine-tuning, commercially available large language models can generate legal-like texts hardly distinguishable from human-created legal texts, although their outputs are overly generic and heavily dependent on the abilities of the human prompter. — general; rests on factual basis. [CLM-0010-006]
- Real-life legislators have already used ChatGPT to draft entire bills: a legislative act written by ChatGPT was adopted by the Porto Alegre city council in Brazil in November 2023, and a Massachusetts state senator turned to ChatGPT to help write a bill regulating artificial intelligence models; at least one of those bills has passed and is now part of a legal system, and these cases are representative of a starting worldwide trend. — BR+US; rests on factual basis. [CLM-0010-007]
- Large language model AI in general, and ChatGPT in particular, generate legal texts without having any intention: generative artificial intelligence models do not possess mental states, dispositions, or attitudes that constitute intentions, and there is no evidence of any internal life in large language models. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0010-008]
- Generative AI models are agents despite lacking mental states or intelligence: they can output relatively autonomous reasonings and even actions if certain preconditions are met, and the outputs of the most advanced large language models can, for all intents and purposes, be compared with the outputs of a human law-creator. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0010-009]
- No technical limitation of large language models has yet been found that would convince the community that the models are, in principle, unable to perform the tasks of law-creation and law-interpretation because of software limitations or hardware bottlenecks. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0010-010]

### Interpretative

- At least three kinds of intention are relevant to law-creation: type intention, the intention to produce an object type such as a rulebook regulating behaviour; token intention, the intention to produce a token of a type with determinate content; and causal intention, the intention that the enacted text cause a certain kind of behaviour. One kind of intention does not necessarily entail the others. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0010-004]
- According to the dominant positions in legal theory and philosophy that make intention a condition of the possibility of law-creation and law-interpretation, the outputs of ChatGPT cannot be considered law: in the lack of anything that might reasonably be called an intention in ChatGPT, its textual outputs resembling legal texts cannot count as law, and a determination of the meaning of the outputs of large language models cannot be considered interpretation. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0010-011]
- When human intervention in the generation of a legal text is limited to formulating the prompt given to an AI, the human type intention and causal intention behind the prompt make the output an intentional creation, but the token intention supposed to be behind the linguistic content of the act is lacking, which eliminates the possibility of law-interpretation based on the actual token intention of the legislator or legal author. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0010-012]
- It is perfectly conceivable to have artefacts without any authorial intention behind them, as long as the use of the artefact attributes and maintains its proper function; within artefact theory, outputs generated by artificial intelligence can be considered legal artefacts as long as they are used as law so that their proper function is the legal regulation of human behaviour. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0010-013]
- The recipients of a text are fully equipped to determine the use functions of the text, confer the necessary legal authority on its maker or generator, and determine the normative content of the text; an intention, real or imagined, can play a role in this determination but is not necessary for the text to give rise to a determinate normative legal meaning. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0010-014]
- Outputs of large language models can count as law on the condition that their function is legal: the ability of agents without intentions, like ChatGPT, to create legal text is an argument in favour of the thesis that law can be created without intention behind the creation and that non-intentional creation can be interpreted to arrive at legal norms. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0010-015]
