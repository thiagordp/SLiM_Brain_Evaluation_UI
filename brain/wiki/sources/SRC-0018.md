# SRC-0018 — Using LLMs to Discover Legal Factors

## What the paper argues

The paper introduces a methodology in which large language models take raw court opinions as input and produce a set of legal factors and associated definitions representing a legal domain, without a pre-existing factor list [CLM-0018-001]. Its motivation is twofold: legal scholars otherwise analyze hundreds of cases by hand, a highly time-consuming and costly process that LLM-based factor discovery could make more efficient [CLM-0018-011], and courts themselves create and apply additional factors as social values or technology change, so canonical factor lists need updating [CLM-0018-010]. On U.S. drug-interdiction reasonable-suspicion cases — a domain where every federal or state decision applies the same Fourth Amendment standard, so cases can come from any U.S. jurisdiction [CLM-0018-013] — zero-shot GPT-4 segmentation of the opinions' analysis and conclusion sections proved reliable with high recall [CLM-0018-004]. A semi-automated approach with a human in the loop produces factor representations that predict case outcomes with moderate success, if not yet as well as expert-defined factors [CLM-0018-002], while fully automated pipelines reach only weak to moderate performance and would need improvement [CLM-0018-003]; LLM-refined factors are sometimes too specific and other times too abstract, which seems to explain their poor predictive power [CLM-0018-006]. LLM annotations made with the expert-defined canonical factor representation came very close to gold-standard predictions, suggesting that representation is robust across annotators [CLM-0018-005]. Both humans and LLMs returned factors that are not legally meaningful — such as an officer's training and experience, which goes to the credibility of the officer's testimony rather than the substance of what the officer says and cannot be used as a factor against the defendant [CLM-0018-012, CLM-0018-007] — underscoring the need to incorporate fundamental legal knowledge into model prompting [CLM-0018-008]. Still, the procedure surfaced a plausible candidate new factor, a driver's use of disguise or deception, showing promise for identifying new factors for pre-defined lists [CLM-0018-009].

## Claims

### Descriptive

- A methodology that leverages large language models can take raw court opinions as input and produce a set of legal factors and associated definitions that effectively represent a legal domain, without using a pre-existing factor list. — US; rests on factual basis. [CLM-0018-001]
- A semi-automated approach in which a large language model induces legal factors from raw opinions with a human in the loop produces factor representations that can predict case outcomes with moderate success, if not yet as well as expert-defined factors can. — US; rests on factual basis. [CLM-0018-002]
- Attempts to fully automate the production of a factor representation of a legal domain from raw opinions using a large language model result in weak to moderate predictive performance; an entirely automated pipeline from raw opinions to a refined factor representation would need improvement and so far performs better with human involvement. — US; rests on factual basis. [CLM-0018-003]
- Zero-shot prompting of a GPT-4 model to identify the span of paragraphs containing a court opinion's analysis and conclusion on the issue of reasonable suspicion is a reliable process, achieving high recall of the factor sets identified by an expert annotator. — US; rests on factual basis. [CLM-0018-004]
- Outcome predictions based on factor sets identified by a large language model using an expert-defined canonical factor representation can come very close in quality to predictions from gold-standard expert annotations, which suggests that the canonical factor representation is robust across annotators. — US; rests on factual basis. [CLM-0018-005]
- Legal factors refined entirely by large language models are sometimes too specific and other times too abstract compared to human-refined factors, and these mismatches and varying levels of generality seem to explain the poor quality of the LLM-refined factor representation and its commensurately poor predictive power. — US; rests on factual basis. [CLM-0018-006]
- Both humans and large language models tasked with inducing legal factors from court opinions return some factors that are not legally meaningful — such as an officer's training and experience, the duration of a traffic stop, or a canine alert — and the failure of large language models to discount frequently appearing language that is contrary to basic legal knowledge highlights their limitations in the legal domain. — US; rests on factual basis. [CLM-0018-007]
- Inducing factors from raw court opinions with large language models shows promise in identifying new factors for pre-defined factor lists: the procedure yielded a plausible candidate — a driver's use of disguise or deception — that could reasonably be categorized as a new factor not on a predefined expert list, or at least as a sub-factor augmenting an existing factor. — US; rests on factual basis. [CLM-0018-009]
- Courts do create and apply additional legal factors beyond canonical lists, even if infrequently; as social values or technology change, courts identify new patterns of case facts whose effect on a decision should be taken into account, and when additionally relevant factors emerge they are often incorporated into the prospective list that subsequent decisions will review. — US; rests on literature. [CLM-0018-010]
- The Fourth Amendment governs the admissibility of evidence in federal and state courts, and state courts follow the guidance of the US Supreme Court in interpreting the amendment; every federal or state decision interpreting reasonable suspicion in a drug interdiction stop relies on the same legal standard, so such cases can come from any jurisdiction in the U.S. — US; rests on legal sources. [CLM-0018-013]

### Interpretative

- An officer's experience and training is not a factor supporting reasonable suspicion: it has to do with the credibility of the officer's testimony, not the substance of what the officer is saying, and a judge, while perhaps more likely to believe a seasoned officer, cannot use the officer's own personal training as a negative factor against the defendant. — US; rests on legal sources. [CLM-0018-012]

### Prescriptive

- There is a need to incorporate fundamental legal knowledge into large language model prompting for more accurate and relevant discovery of legal factors. — US; rests on factual basis. [CLM-0018-008]

### Predictive

- In the absence of predefined factors from courts or legislative bodies, legal scholars manually analyze hundreds of cases to identify factors, a process that is highly time-consuming and costly; large-language-model-based factor discovery could enable a more efficient process of identifying factor representations of legal domain cases. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0018-011]
