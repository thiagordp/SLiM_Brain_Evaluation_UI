# SRC-0005 — Encoded Normativity: Designing Large Language Models for International Law

## What the paper argues

The paper holds that the architectural choices required to fine-tune a large language model for international law — selecting the training data, integrating human expertise for reinforcement learning, and designing the user interface — embed jurisprudential assumptions and force designers to take a stance on the nature of international law and legal interpretation [CLM-0005-001], and that the most transformative aspect of legal LLMs may be precisely this demand for positioning rather than their computational capabilities [CLM-0005-003]. Its central hypothesis is that these choices should be made from the user's point of view [CLM-0005-002], in a literature where the designer perspective on LLMs for international law is very scarce [CLM-0005-004]. On data, it argues that the Article 38(1) ICJ Statute framework is arguably neither entirely clear nor exhaustive [CLM-0005-005], that determining the fine-tuning dataset requires decisions on contested sources questions [CLM-0005-006], that the user's perspective and representativity are the two parameters for that decision [CLM-0005-007], and that the design space runs on a continuum between a narrow, sources-strict approach and a broad, inclusive one, each with its costs [CLM-0005-008]. On human feedback, it argues that reinforcement learning is fundamentally a debate about legal interpretation [CLM-0005-009], that interpretation in international law is a decentralised, contested and value-laden practice [CLM-0005-010], that fine-tuning requires a participative approach conceptualising representativity on the two axes of interpreters' biographies and justificatory values [CLM-0005-011], and that whether fine-tuning targets majority or minority interpretations should follow the user's goal [CLM-0005-012]. On the interface, it argues that interpretative disagreements are inherent features of law, not flaws for an LLM to solve [CLM-0005-013], that the design axis runs from single-answer to panorama designs and reflects normative commitments about representing disagreement [CLM-0005-014], that interface design is a promising venue to cabin hallucinations, which are integral to a probabilistic tool [CLM-0005-015], and that a legal LLM should communicate its reasoning stages, adapt to the user and use counterfactual logic [CLM-0005-016]. It predicts that users' trust in LLMs has the potential to redefine what people consider international law [CLM-0005-017], and concludes that benchmarks must add the user's context and law as a rich interpretative practice [CLM-0005-018], that benchmarking should be understood as a normative exercise [CLM-0005-020], and that the idea of one or a few LLMs for international law should be abandoned in favour of a configurable variety [CLM-0005-019].

## Claims

### Descriptive

- The literature at the junction of designing a large language model, capable of addressing real-world legal questions, in international law is very scarce, and the designer perspective on legal large language models is under-researched in current scholarship. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0005-004]
- Determining the dataset used to fine-tune an international-law large language model — and the content of a retrieval-augmented generation database — requires taking decisions on contested questions about the sources of international law, including which customs should be used, whether and which international and national case-law is relevant, and whether soft law resources should be included; for the model to provide reliable outputs, the dataset must reflect a coherent and representative understanding of these sources. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0005-006]

### Interpretative

- The architectural choices required to fine-tune a large language model for international law — selecting the domain-specific training data, integrating human expertise for reinforcement learning, and designing the user interface — embed jurisprudential assumptions and require designers to take a stance on fundamental questions concerning the nature of international law and legal interpretation. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0005-001]
- Although the framework of Article 38(1) of the ICJ Statute is still regarded as the cornerstone of international legal methodology, the formal sources of international law it identifies are arguably neither entirely clear nor exhaustive. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0005-005]
- Two parameters are important for navigating the design decision about the content of the dataset used to fine-tune an international-law large language model: the perspective of the user and the question of representativity, as key features of what constitutes 'good' international law. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0005-007]
- The choice of fine-tuning data and retrieval-augmented generation database for an international-law large language model can be represented on a continuum between a narrow, restrictive approach based on a strict reading of the sources of Article 38(1) ICJ Statute — which offers clarity, coherence and predictability but risks perpetuating existing structural features of international law and excluding voices that challenge the status quo — and a broad, inclusive approach incorporating diverse perspectives — which enhances representativity but may lack the formal authority and consistency required for real-world application. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0005-008]
- Reinforcement learning from human feedback in the context of training large language models for international law is fundamentally a debate about legal interpretation: providing feedback to the machine requires taking a position on what it means to interpret international law in general and specific legal concepts in particular. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0005-009]
- Interpretation in international law is best captured as a decentralised and contested practice involving States, international courts, international organisations and non-State actors, and applying the VCLT's methods of interpretation (Articles 31-33) to ascribe meaning cannot be compared to a mechanistic, value-free process. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0005-010]
- Interpretative disagreements over the meaning of legal norms are not flaws or errors to be 'solved' by a large language model but are inherent features of the law and of the interpretation it requires. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0005-013]
- The spectrum of user-interface design choices for a legal large language model can be conceptualised along an axis between a single-answer design and a panorama design presenting a range of plausible interpretations, and the choice reflects deeper normative commitments about how legal disagreement should be represented and about who — the designer of the LLM or the user — bears responsibility for taking a decision considering those disagreements. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0005-014]
- Because hallucinations are an integral part of the operation of a probabilistic tool rather than a marginal phenomenon or a technical problem, and their consequences can be serious in law, the design of the user interface is a promising venue to cabin and mitigate hallucinations. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0005-015]
- Rather than a mere descriptive performance assessment, benchmarking of legal large language models should be understood as a normative exercise, one that shapes how international law is represented and operationalised within LLMs. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0005-020]

### Prescriptive

- Architectural design choices for a large language model for international law should be made by adopting the user's point of view, with 'what is the LLM for, from the perspective of the user?' as the key question guiding design decisions. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0005-002]
- The fine-tuning of a legal large language model requires a participative approach in which representativity is conceptualised on at least two axes — the biographies of the interpreters and the values used to justify substantial interpretations — and, along a critical participatory design approach, marginalised profiles should be proactively integrated. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0005-011]
- If the user of an international-law large language model is looking for answers as to what is allowed or prohibited, its fine-tuning should be oriented towards a majority position on the interpretation of key legal norms; if the user is looking for frontier interpretations of specific norms, the reinforcement learning should guide the machine to consider minority interpretations. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0005-012]
- A legal large language model should communicate its different stages of reasoning, including which information is grounded in the retrieval database, and its main interpretative choices; it must be configurable to adapt its explanations to the user's mental model, level of knowledge, abilities and needs; and its interface should use counterfactual logic, identifying interpretative crossroads that lead to different results. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0005-016]
- Any benchmark trying to capture (international) legal work should add two layers: the relevance of the context of the users and the promotion of the law as a rich interpretative practice; a large language model cannot be benchmarked in a vacuum, and its performance must be measured on the basis of a specific type of user and context. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0005-018]
- The idea of having one or a few large language models for international law should be abandoned; instead, a variety of LLMs should be developed, or a system able to be configured according to distinct sets of data and interpretations of key legal norms. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0005-019]

### Predictive

- The most transformative aspect of legal large language models may not be their computational capabilities, but their demand for positioning on the nature and function of legal interpretation in international law. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0005-003]
- The trust placed in large language models by users — especially models developed by authoritative entities — has the potential to redefine what and how people consider international law; if users rely on these models as definitive interpretative machines, the influence of their design choices will ripple across legal systems and international decision-making processes. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0005-017]
