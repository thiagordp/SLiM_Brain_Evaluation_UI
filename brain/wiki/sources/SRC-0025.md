# SRC-0025 — Contract Law Illusions and Delusions

## What the paper argues

The essay argues that much of what lawyers believe about contract law emanates from illusions and delusions embedded in the doctrinal canon, such as 'the meeting of the minds' and 'the document speaks for itself' [CLM-0025-001]. The central micro-illusion is 'mutual intention': there is no such thing as a mutual intention of the parties in a colorable interpretation dispute, and no shared mind that can be known rather than posited by belief [CLM-0025-002] — a trope to which even text-focused theorists and the proponents of LLM-based generative interpretation pay lip service while their own theses undercut it [CLM-0025-005]. There is no 'correct answer' at which interpretation should aim; litigation is sublimated violence whose result is as likely a crapshoot as anything else [CLM-0025-003], so parties should accept randomness and uncertainty when deciding whether to litigate or settle [CLM-0025-011]. Large language models, which know nothing about contracts and predict probable next words without understanding semantics [CLM-0025-006], nonetheless fit contract law well: meaning in law is disembodied community meaning, and generative interpretation is that meaning derived digitally at vast scale [CLM-0025-007]; the third-party interpreter can be an LLM, which belies the need for the shared-intention illusion and yields a result more satisfying than a coin flip [CLM-0025-008]. The academic text-versus-context debate is a false dichotomy that sensible courts already reject [CLM-0025-009], and LLMs may be the adjudicatory technology that resolves it at the lawyering scale [CLM-0025-010], though the uncertainty may be so great that LLM use makes no difference to outcome certainty [CLM-0025-012]. At the macro level, there is a dearth of empirical evidence that the institution of contract law makes any difference [CLM-0025-013]; against the three established scholarly accounts of contracting [CLM-0025-014], the essay proposes a fourth: contracts are meaningful because people think they are — delusions of order, means of groping for order, reassurance and certainty in the face of chaos [CLM-0025-015], resting on 'dark trust', the unnoticed, unmeasurable expectation of predictable behavior that defies science and legal theory [CLM-0025-016]. Contract-making is ritual and self-actualization more than textual logic [CLM-0025-004]; a 'science' of contract law claiming to explain all transacting behavior is delusional [CLM-0025-017]; and the sooner the illusions of provable mutual intentions are discarded, the better off contracting parties will be, since interpretation is communal — there are no private languages [CLM-0025-018].

## Claims

### Descriptive

- Some of what lawyers, both academic and practicing, believe about contract law emanates from illusions and delusions embedded in and around the doctrinal canon — phrases like 'the meeting of the minds' or 'the document speaks for itself' — which are not easily dismissed as mere metaphors and persist because it is difficult to demarcate myth from history or belief from knowledge. — US; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0025-001]
- Even theorists whose work justifies a text-focused approach to contract interpretation, such as Alan Schwartz and Robert Scott, and the proponents of LLM-based generative interpretation pay lip service to the 'mutual intention' or 'shared intention of the parties' trope even as their own theses undercut it. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0025-005]
- A large language model does not know anything about contracts or contract theory and does not understand the semantics of what it receives or produces; like the person in Searle's Chinese Room, it is trained to predict the probable next words in a sentence from patterns of word usage in huge volumes of text. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0025-006]
- There is a dearth of empirical evidence whether the legal institution of contract law makes any difference to investment, morality, or a better life, and the quantitative and qualitative research that does exist is at best unresolved on that question. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0025-013]
- Established scholarly accounts of why people engage in contracting practices fall into three categories: contracts are very meaningful because they enhance economic welfare (lawyers and economists starting from rational-actor axioms or from bounded rationality); contracts are very meaningful because they embody in law the moral principle that people should honor their promises; and contracts are not nearly as meaningful as thought (work at the intersection of law and sociology). — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0025-014]

### Interpretative

- 'Mutual intention' and its corresponding metaphor 'the meeting of the minds' are metaphysical nonsense: there is no such thing as a mutual intention of the parties in a colorable contract interpretation dispute, and no shared mind or 'shared intention' thought bubble that can be known rather than posited by belief. — US; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0025-002]
- There is no 'correct answer' at which the exercise of contract interpretation should aim; parties who submit an interpretation dispute to a third party rather than settling have gone to war in a form of sublimated violence, the result of which is as likely a crapshoot as anything else. — US; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0025-003]
- The practice of making contracts has less to do with the logic of the text than it does with ritual, self-actualization, and the unrequited human desire for certainty; contracts are rituals that help avoid violence as a means of conflict resolution and serve higher orders of self-fulfillment. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0025-004]
- Meaning in law, as distinguished from meaning in fact, is a disembodied meaning derived from the community; LLM-based generative interpretation of contracts is just that, derived digitally rather than through the analog musings of a judge or jury, and the data it operates on is community meaning on a vastly larger scale — millions or billions of instances of community word usage rather than the particular usages of the contract parties. — US; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0025-007]
- The third party who observes and understands the objective manifestations of contractual agreement can be a large language model, even though it is a proxy for a human mind and does not think like one; this belies the need for the shared-intention illusion, and since 'shared intention' can never be known, the result will be more satisfying as a rational result than a mere coin flip or roll of the dice. — US; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0025-008]
- The academic text-versus-context debate in contract interpretation is a false dichotomy: a practicing lawyer argues as a textualist or a contextualist according to the client's interest in the dispute, and sensible courts reject binary absolutism, sometimes following the text and sometimes looking at the context as common sense dictates. — US; rests on legal sources. [CLM-0025-009]
- A fourth, contrarian account of contracting: contracts are meaningful because people think they are and rely on them like monkey-ropes, even if that reliance is something of a delusion — contracts, rather than being very meaningful or not nearly as meaningful as one thought, are means of groping for order, reassurance, fulfillment, and certainty in the face of chaos, insecurity, and the unknown unknowns; they can be delusions of order. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0025-015]
- Humans operate within a universe of social norms the vast majority of which is 'dark trust' — an expectation of predictable behavior about which human beings are rarely if ever conscious, and which defies explanation by way of science or legal theory; the very ability to cope with the world entails dark trust that people barely notice and do not have the means to measure. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0025-016]
- The idea that the logic of a 'science' of contract law can explain all transacting behavior is delusional — as delusional as a physicist insisting on baryonic matter as the end-all of cosmology; contract practice, in contrast to the economists' delusion of a 'complete contract', incorporates all kinds of apparently irrational practices based on beliefs that may well be illusions or delusions. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0025-017]

### Prescriptive

- Parties should accept randomness and uncertainty as part of their calculation when deciding whether to litigate or settle a contract dispute; one effect of the misguided focus on shared intention is that parties approach litigation with the idea of vindicating the right answer, rather than accepting that it is a crapshoot based on somebody's reconstruction of the parties' objective manifestations. — US; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0025-011]
- The sooner the illusions of provable mutual intentions are discarded and the delusions that contracts are anything other than imperfect maps on a highly complex reality put aside, the better off contracting parties will be; it takes a village to interpret the signs people make to each other, because there are no private languages and the words and phrases are borrowed from the community in an attempt to be understood by way of objective manifestations. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0025-018]

### Predictive

- Large language models may be the adjudicatory technology that resolves the text-versus-context issue at the practical lawyering scale where it matters: as the technology improves, judges can relax old safeguards towards a more inclusive interpretive approach. — US; rests on literature. [CLM-0025-010]
- As to whether the use of large language models in contract interpretation will make outcomes more or less certain and thereby reduce litigation by checking opportunism, the uncertainty may be so great that it does not make any difference, and trying to limit opportunism is a fool's game. — US; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0025-012]
