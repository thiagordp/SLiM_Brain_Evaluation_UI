# SRC-0026 — Challenges for generative AI in legal reasoning

## What the paper argues

The paper starts from the observation that the limitations of LLMs in high-stakes fields such as law remain poorly understood, in particular where their boundaries lie across domains [CLM-0026-001], and deconstructs the core challenges AI must overcome to function reliably in judicial decision-making: selecting the correct legal framework, arguing from the doctrine of the sources of law, distinguishing ratio decidendi from obiter dicta, resolving general-clause ambiguity, managing conflicting provisions, and applying the burden of proof [CLM-0026-002]. The judicial process is bound by imperatives — impartiality, legal certainty, transparency, justifiability — that are functional requirements of a legitimate legal system [CLM-0026-003], and AI's greatest challenges lie in the rule-selection and application phases of legal reasoning [CLM-0026-004]. Specific challenges are worked through in turn: cross-border framework selection and connecting factors [CLM-0026-011], the simultaneous constraints of hierarchy, time and specificity within one legal system [CLM-0026-012], the insufficiency of interpretation maxims and the inability of traditional symbolic AI to interpret [CLM-0026-013], sources-of-law reasoning over preparatory works and amendments that current LLMs often lack the higher-order capability for [CLM-0026-014], ratio decidendi extraction and legally relevant analogy [CLM-0026-010], general clauses that neither retrieval nor agentic systems can solve [CLM-0026-009], gap-filling that requires tracking dependencies across many laws and principles [CLM-0026-015], the continuous interaction of facts and rules [CLM-0026-016], evidence evaluation that RAG and fine-tuning cannot improve [CLM-0026-005] because LLMs lack lived experience and common-sense grounding [CLM-0026-006], and burden-of-proof application undermined by the poor calibration and overconfidence of LLMs [CLM-0026-007]; an AI deciding on insufficient evidence would undermine procedural justice [CLM-0026-008]. Procedural fairness requires the generative process behind any AI output used in adjudication — down to the prompt — to be transparent and open to challenge [CLM-0026-017], with audit trails, disclosure and human oversight as design requirements [CLM-0026-019]. Mapping AI mechanisms to these challenges, no single technique is a panacea: techniques address narrow challenges but fail the significant ones requiring discretion and justifiable reasoning [CLM-0026-018]; a neuro-symbolic system is nonetheless feasible for high-volume, low-complexity small claims [CLM-0026-021]. Legal AI should be evaluated with domain-specific metrics rather than linguistic ones [CLM-0026-020], within a framework that organises requirements into four categories and operationalises them as testable design obligations [CLM-0026-022]. The paper concludes with a dual-application model — AI as high-volume assistant for simple cases and sparring partner for experts [CLM-0026-023] — and advocates staged adoption [CLM-0026-024], since LLMs excel where legal tasks reduce to retrieval and pattern recognition but a fundamental barrier remains between their probabilistic nature and choice-driven judicial reasoning [CLM-0026-025].

## Claims

### Descriptive

- The limitations of large language models in high-stakes fields such as law remain poorly understood; while it is well understood that LLMs have severe limitations in their ability to reason and produce truthful responses, where the boundaries of these limitations lie across domains is less well understood. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0026-001]
- For AI to function reliably in judicial decision-making, it must overcome a set of core challenges: selecting the correct legal framework across jurisdictions, generating sound arguments based on the doctrine of the sources of law, distinguishing ratio decidendi from obiter dicta in case law, resolving ambiguity arising from general clauses such as 'reasonableness', managing conflicting legal provisions, and applying the burden of proof correctly. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-002]
- While AI can be a powerful tool in the initial issue-discovery phase of legal reasoning, its greatest challenges and potential lie in the two demanding phases of selecting the correct rule and applying it to the facts of the case, where the highest standards of judicial reasoning are required. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-004]
- Evidence evaluation is difficult to improve with techniques like retrieval-augmented generation because legal evidence pertains to the truthfulness of everyday facts: fine-tuning an LLM with legal corpora does not help it answer whether a certain person was in a certain place at a certain time, and LLMs struggle to evaluate the truthfulness of real-world facts, a task often reliant on human experience and credibility assessments. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-005]
- LLMs face a fundamental challenge in evaluating the truthfulness of information such as evidence, as their knowledge is derived from training data and provided context, not from lived experience or an innate sense of real-world plausibility; assessing witness credibility involves non-verbal cues, demeanor, consistency and potential biases that extend far beyond a written transcript, and evaluating real-world plausibility requires common-sense reasoning, an area in which LLMs continue to show significant limitations. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0026-006]
- Applying the burden of proof correctly requires a decision-maker first to assess its own level of certainty and recognize when the evidence is insufficient to meet the required threshold, which points to a critical technical limitation of current models: LLMs are notoriously poor at this form of self-assessment, their confidence scores are often misaligned with their actual accuracy — a problem known as poor calibration — and models frequently exhibit overconfidence. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0026-007]
- To apply general clauses — legal rules deliberately formulated in an imprecise manner using open-textured terms like 'reasonable', 'fair' or 'unconscionable' — AI cannot be a mere legal formalist: an understanding of social norms, ethics and common sense is required; such clauses are especially challenging for LLMs and cannot be solved simply with better information retrieval, as there is no reference material for general clauses in every context, nor by agentic systems, because the open-textured terminology cannot be broken down into well-defined factors. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-009]
- Distinguishing the ratio decidendi from obiter dicta and conducting a legally relevant comparison of the facts between a precedent and the current case is one of the most difficult tasks for AI: the ratio decidendi is an abstract legal concept, not an explicit piece of text, legal analogy is an evaluative judgment about whether the facts of two cases are comparable in legally relevant ways, and current AI systems, which excel at identifying semantic patterns in text, struggle to make the independent, evaluative judgments needed to formulate or apply the underlying normative principles. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-010]
- In cross-border disputes, AI must first distinguish between jurisdiction and applicable law and apply international instruments such as the Brussels I Recast Regulation and the Rome I Regulation; while knowledge retrieval systems can find the relevant regulations, AI systems may struggle to interpret the connecting factors that determine jurisdiction, which normally allow for judicial discretion and context-sensitive application, and a multi-agent decomposition still relies on the agents' ability to interpret the web of logical dependencies correctly. — general, EU; rests on legal sources (Regulation (EU) 1215/2012 (Brussels I Recast); Regulation (EC) 593/2008 (Rome I)). [CLM-0026-011]
- Choosing the right provision within a single country's legal system is a multi-layered task in which an AI system must simultaneously respect the hierarchy of norms, the temporal scope of law, the maxim lex specialis derogat legi generali, and the correct internal procedural choice; this systemic reasoning challenge is difficult to implement with simple prompts, and while a neuro-symbolic AI system could perhaps model hierarchies of statutes, defining all the rules and their internal relations is a monumental undertaking. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-012]
- A reliable AI must construct its legal reasoning by starting with the literal wording of the provision and interpreting it in light of the legislator's purpose, sourcing that purpose from preparatory works and verifying whether Parliament amended the final law; this demands a structured comparison and logical inferencing about textual differences between legislative drafts, a higher-order reasoning capability that current LLMs often lack. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-014]
- In cases of a lacuna in law, AI must be able to reason via analogies from related provisions or derive a solution from general principles; adjudicating without the support of a rule requires a deep, systemic understanding of the entire legal framework, and while an LLM can process vast amounts of text, it struggles to track the dependencies and interactions across many factors simultaneously — a critical point of failure for AI. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0026-015]
- Establishing the relevant facts of a case is a precondition for identifying the legal issue and determining the applicable law; issues of evidence and law may be intertwined, evidence presented during proceedings can change the applicable rules, and this continuous interaction between facts and rules can be difficult for AI to follow. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-016]
- No single AI technique is a panacea for the demands of legal reasoning; a combination of approaches is required, and while techniques such as retrieval-augmented generation, multi-agent systems and neuro-symbolic AI can address specific narrow challenges, they fail to solve the more significant ones that remain, particularly in tasks requiring discretion and transparent, justifiable reasoning. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0026-018]
- The current generation of LLMs excels when legal tasks can be distilled into sophisticated information retrieval and pattern recognition, serving best with abundant reference material, clearly defined concepts and decomposable sub-tasks; when these conditions are not met, a fundamental barrier remains — the gap between the probabilistic nature of language models and the principled, choice-driven nature of judicial reasoning. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-025]

### Interpretative

- The judicial process is bound by a set of imperatives — impartiality, legal certainty, transparency, and the justifiability and controllability of every step of decision-making — which are not mere aspirations but functional requirements of a legitimate legal system, largely common regardless of how legal argumentation is theoretically constructed. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-003]
- An AI system that attempts to render a decision based on insufficient evidence, rather than correctly identifying the issue as 'unproven' and applying burden of proof rules, would fundamentally undermine procedural justice. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-008]
- Legal interpretation is not a matter of deductive logic but of constructing a persuasive argument grounded in accepted sources of law, so traditional symbolic AI is unable to perform interpretation tasks; unwritten interpretation maxims can be helpful when integrated into LLM prompting and agent architecture, but alone they are insufficient tools in legal adjudication and vary across legal systems. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-013]
- The principle of procedural fairness dictates that if an AI's output is to be used as evidence or to support a judicial decision, the process that generated it must be transparent and open to challenge: the specific prompt becomes discoverable evidence, and the choice of AI model is a methodological decision comparable to an expert witness selecting a scientific instrument. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-017]
- Traditional NLP metrics like BLEU measure linguistic properties rather than meaning and are insufficient for legal tasks; domain-specific evaluation metrics reflecting core competencies in legal reasoning — statute recall, ratio decidendi identification accuracy, provenance coverage, burden-of-proof handling — are being defined. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0026-020]
- A neuro-symbolic system is a feasible approach to high-volume, low-complexity disputes such as consumer product-defect small claims: an LLM observation engine proposes structured facts while a symbolic layer performs the determinative legal reasoning, delivering coverage over unstructured inputs and transparent, auditable decisions. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-021]
- The most effective current role for AI in law is a dual-application model: a high-volume assistant for simple cases — where LLMs can already work well with today's techniques — and a sophisticated 'sparring partner' for experts in complex matters, where the LLM is a collaborator, not an adjudicator. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-023]

### Prescriptive

- AI-assisted adjudication must include system-design features for procedural fairness: audit trails recording all inputs, outputs and intermediate reasoning steps as a discoverable record — a legal necessity if AI outputs are to be contestable evidence in court; disclosure of the AI's role; and contestability with human oversight and override. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-019]
- A framework for judicial AI should organize requirements into four categories — normative and procedural values; doctrinal and reasoning constraints; fact-finding and evidential requirements; system-level technical properties — scope them to concrete legal domains, specify operational design obligations, and make them testable, with deployment conditional on minimum metric thresholds. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-022]
- Adoption of AI in adjudication should be staged: first capturing efficiency in simple cases with technology available today, thereafter sustaining long-term investment in methods that handle hierarchy, temporality and other requirements of legally sound reasoning, enabling expansion to complex adjudication. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0026-024]
