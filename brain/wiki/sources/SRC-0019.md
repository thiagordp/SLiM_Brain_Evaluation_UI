# SRC-0019 — Against AI Jurisprudence: Large Language Models and the False Promises of Empirical Judging

## What the paper argues

Against proposals that judges use large language models to find the 'ordinary meaning' of legal text, the paper argues that LLMs would not remove human subjectivity from interpretation but merely shift the source of bias to other parts of the process, such as model developers' design decisions — and that without a consistent prompting method they would not reduce discretionary choices at all [CLM-0019-001]. LLMs cannot solve the central problem dogging all legal interpretation, that judges must make the normative, often outcome-determinative choice of which context counts [CLM-0019-005]. More fundamentally, determining a legal text's ordinary meaning is not an empirical project: ordinary meaning is a value-laden construct, no more fact-based than the search for congressional intent [CLM-0019-002], and the proof of concept offered for asking GPT the ordinary meaning of statutory terms is read as a reconstruction of prior human survey results rather than an empirical exploration [CLM-0019-006]. Crowdsourcing legal meaning also lacks democratic legitimacy: substituting a polling proxy — human or LLM — for a judge's individual reasoning is not the process the US Constitution mandates, and a judge who handed over the ultimate decision would violate her duty to decide the case [CLM-0019-003]; pushed too far, the seemingly democratic empirical aspect of textualism undermines the method's political legitimacy, a problem general to textualism that LLMs make more visible [CLM-0019-004]. LLMs are not a democratic technology as currently manufactured, and letting technocratic elites' engineering decisions influence courts is undemocratic [CLM-0019-012]. The paper predicts that LLM use will exacerbate textualism's 'scientific' veneer while obscuring normative choices [CLM-0019-007], that prompt engineering and 'LLM shopping' will become the new 'dictionary shopping', with confirmation bias and politically motivated reasoning the most likely outcome [CLM-0019-010], and that the technology will not solve the fundamental trade-off between judicial discretion and rule-bound consistency [CLM-0019-011]. It prescribes that LLM outputs be treated as no more definitive than other evidence of ordinary meaning and never be used to decide the core interpretive questions at issue [CLM-0019-008], and that LLMs not be used for inherently normative tasks such as judging, values being better chosen explicitly than accepted hidden from technology companies [CLM-0019-009].

## Claims

### Descriptive

- Even if judges consistently used a single prompting method, large language models would not remove human subjectivity from determinations of the ordinary meaning of legal text: they shift the source of bias to other parts of the process, such as model developers' design decisions; and in the more likely scenario of no consistent prompting method, using LLMs will not reduce the role of discretionary choices at all. — US; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0019-001]
- Large language models cannot solve a central problem that dogs all legal interpretation — that judges must decide which relevant context to take into account when defining the meaning of words; deciding which context is relevant to interpreting a particular legal term is a normative choice, often undiscussed, and likely to be outcome determinative. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0019-005]

### Interpretative

- The determination of a legal text's ordinary meaning is not an empirical project: ordinary meaning is a value-laden construct defined in part by the interpreter's judgment of what is reasonable, and textualist analysis of ordinary meaning is no more empirical or fact-based than the judicial search for congressional intent. — US; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0019-002]
- Substituting a polling proxy — a panel drawn from the public or a large language model prediction of aggregated public opinion — for a judge's individual reasoning is not the process mandated for legal decisions by the text of the US Constitution and by US legal culture; a judge who handed over her ultimate decision to such a proxy would be violating her duty to decide the case. — US; rests on legal sources. [CLM-0019-003]
- Pushed too far, the very aspect of textualism that seems to promise greater democratic legitimacy — decision-making based on an empirical investigation of ordinary people's understanding of legal text — undermines the interpretive method's political legitimacy; the problem is general to all textualist interpretation but becomes more visible when large language models are used for textualist analysis. — US; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0019-004]
- The proof of concept offered for asking GPT the ordinary meaning of statutory terms (Engel and McAdams) does not look like an empirical exploration: the LLM inquiries seem to have been engineered to carefully reconstruct results previously obtained from an empirical survey of actual humans, with the outcome viewed as objectively correct chosen in advance. — US; rests on literature. [CLM-0019-006]
- Large language models are not a democratic technology, at least as currently manufactured, and there is something decidedly undemocratic about allowing the engineering decisions of a few technocratic elites to influence the operation of the courts. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0019-012]

### Prescriptive

- Large language models should be thought of as no more definitive than other sources of 'evidence' of ordinary meaning, such as dictionaries, must only be used with an understanding of their limits, their tendencies to hallucinate and their sensitivity to context and priming, and must never be used to decide the core interpretive questions at issue in a case. — US; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0019-008]
- Large language models should not be used for inherently normative tasks such as judging: normative values are always present in the legal process, and it is better to be explicit and choose socially desirable values than to accept without question the hidden and often harmful normative values forced on us by technology companies. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0019-009]

### Predictive

- Given the prevalence of AI hype, the use of large language models threatens to exacerbate textualism's 'scientific' quality, suggesting reproducibility and neutrality while obscuring the ever-present normative choices that go into legal interpretation. — US; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0019-007]
- Prompt engineering and 'LLM shopping' are anticipated to become the new 'dictionary shopping': confirmation bias and politically motivated reasoning is the most likely outcome of increased adoption of large language models for legal interpretation, and empirical research on judges' use of these new interpretive methods is needed. — US; rests on literature. [CLM-0019-010]
- However the legal world ultimately decides to use large language models, this technology is not going to solve the fundamental issue of trading off between judicial discretion and more rule-bound consistency. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0019-011]
