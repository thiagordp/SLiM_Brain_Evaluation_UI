# SRC-0006 — Pseudolaw and the illusion of legal meaning

## What the paper argues

The paper holds that pseudolaw — the social phenomenon whereby adherents use the forms of conventional legal argument without its substance [CLM-0006-003] — rests, at its heart, on a mistaking of the form of legal argumentation for its substance, an illusion of legal meaning that tricks users into believing their actions are legally meaningful [CLM-0006-001]. Pseudolaw arguments have no legal merit and do not work [CLM-0006-004], yet they have grown significantly since the COVID-19 pandemic and now occur daily in Australian courts [CLM-0006-005], with each case disproportionately burdening the courts [CLM-0006-006]. The paper juxtaposes pseudolaw with generative AI: both produce an illusion of meaning that users mistake for actual meaning, replicating form but not substance [CLM-0006-002], because large language models predict words as vectors with no understanding, external verification of meaning or intentionality [CLM-0006-013]; the two phenomena are beginning to collide in the courts [CLM-0006-012]. The illusion succeeds because law itself is archaic and inaccessible [CLM-0006-010] and pseudolaw flourishes on alienation and lack of legal literacy [CLM-0006-011], through mechanisms of conceptual pareidolia [CLM-0006-014], the confidence heuristic [CLM-0006-015], form-driven success [CLM-0006-016] and magical thinking [CLM-0006-017]. Responses should meet it with pragmatic disinterest, vary by user type and disrupt its performance [CLM-0006-018]; unchecked, and turbocharged by generative AI, it could become an existential threat to legal processes [CLM-0006-020].

## Claims

### Descriptive

- Pseudolaw arguments are without any legal merit and do not work; courts have rejected them as 'obvious nonsense', 'pseudo-legal gibberish' and an unnecessary waste of scarce public and judicial resources. — AU; rests on legal sources. [CLM-0006-004]
- Pseudolaw arguments have proliferated, with significant growth in the years since the COVID-19 pandemic; pseudolaw matters now occur on a daily basis in Australian courts and in some jurisdictions are already seen to profoundly reshape litigation. — AU; rests on literature. [CLM-0006-005]
- Pseudolaw is particularly disruptive to courts: each pseudolaw case has a disproportionate impact compared to other cases, and the burden of dealing with pseudolaw matters is having a profound impact on the courts. — AU; rests on literature. [CLM-0006-006]
- In Australia and Aotearoa New Zealand, pseudolegal argumentation falls into three broad categories: the strawman argument (the law does not apply because it applies only to 'artificial' persons possessing a separate legal personality), absence of individual consent (government authority is illegitimate absent individual consent), and state law is defective (the law was invalidly enacted and is of no legal effect). — AU+NZ; rests on literature. [CLM-0006-008]
- Pseudolaw is beginning to interact with the widespread use of generative AI, particularly large language models, in a particularly alarming manner: a litigant in person has used an LLM to generate real-time responses to questions from the bench while advancing pseudolaw-like arguments, and instances of written submissions containing clear hallucinations are becoming common. — AU; rests on factual basis. [CLM-0006-012]
- Large language models make predictions about the most likely next word in a sequence by treating each word as a number ('words-as-vectors'); they make no attempt to understand or externally validate the meaning of the terms used — all that matters is statistical closeness — so their outputs have the appearance of meaning while there is no external verification of meaning and no underlying intentionality in the use of words. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0006-013]

### Interpretative

- Pseudolaw involves, at its heart, a mistaking of the form of legal argumentation for its substance: through the use of legalistic language, archaic sources and strange rituals, an illusion of legal meaning is created which tricks users into believing their actions are legally meaningful. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0006-001]
- Pseudolaw shares common features with generative AI: both produce an illusion of meaning which users mistake for actual meaning, both succeed in replicating the form of their mimicked output but not its substance, and both flourish where their users desperately wish to believe in the efficacy of the output. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0006-002]
- Pseudolaw is the social phenomenon whereby adherents use the forms of conventional legal argument, but not the substance, in their interactions with law, legal and government institutions; it co-opts the form of legal language while substituting its own norms and contra-narratives, creating an alternative legal universe that adherents believe represents the 'true' meaning of the law. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0006-003]
- The Sovereign Citizen movement, which emerged in the United States in the 1990s, is perhaps the most significant and influential sect of pseudolaw, but conflating it with the broader phenomenon of pseudolaw is a mistake: a wide range of movements, groups and practices share a common methodological approach to engaging with the law. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0006-007]
- Rather than a focus simply on the style and content of the arguments used, pseudolaw is best understood through the behaviours and attitudes of its followers — a conspiratorial worldview, constant questioning of authority, proliferation of irrelevant and voluminous filings, and scripted, often illogical arguments — with theatricality and ritualistic behaviour among its defining features. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0006-009]
- Because the law uses archaic language and often Byzantine ritual and is conceptually inaccessible to the general member of the public, it is far too easy for an alienated and excluded layperson to mistake pseudolegal behaviour that mimics the form of law for law itself; excluding the population from meaningful engagement with the law robs them of the ability to distinguish between law and not-law. — AU; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0006-010]
- A sense of alienation and legal powerlessness underlies the emergence of pseudolaw; pseudolaw flourishes where a lack of legal literacy — the ability to meaningfully utilise and understand law and legal processes — collides with an individual's need to engage with legal institutions, and it thrives where alienated individuals desperately want to believe in the illusion of law it creates. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0006-011]
- Users of pseudolaw generally hold a genuine belief that what they are doing represents actual law because they are experiencing a kind of pareidolia — the human tendency to perceive meaningful patterns in unrelated stimuli: the ritualistic words appear so like how they expect law to look that they think they are looking at law. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0006-014]
- Users of pseudolaw are notably more confident in the presentation of their arguments than other litigants in person; this may be a consequence of users mistaking confidence for substantive knowledge, drawing on the heuristic of treating confidence as a proxy for knowledge (the 'confidence heuristic'): the very confidence with which pseudolegal materials mimic legal forms makes adherents more likely to believe that what is presented is 'true law'. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0006-015]
- Pseudolaw and large language models can both be highly successful in a certain way, provided the relevant domain focuses on form rather than substance; for pseudolaw, this form-as-success helps explain both the communicative expertise developed by some users and the role of gurus who sell access to the specialised knowledge and language forms they purport to possess — a success of form and not substance. — general; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0006-016]
- Pseudolaw appears to offer something deeply desirable — genuine, empowering access to specialised knowledge achieved through special ritualised forms; magical thinking is at the heart of most sovereign citizen pseudolaw, and recognition of the appeal of this magical thinking by users is vital if judges and educators are to help users abandon the practice. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0006-017]
- Pseudolaw is the logical extension of 50 years of diminishing access to justice and a chronic underinvestment in civics and legal literacy education; failures in those domains have given rise to pseudolaw, which is already profoundly disrupting courts. — AU; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0006-019]

### Prescriptive

- In responding to pseudolaw, both mockery and engagement should be avoided — pragmatic disinterest in the argument, but not the person, is the preferable approach; responses should vary depending on the type of user confronted (the naive, the true believer, the mercenary and the guru); and, because the form and performance of pseudolaw matters, the most successful responses are aimed at disrupting the performance. — general; rests on literature. [CLM-0006-018]

### Predictive

- If turbocharged through its interaction with generative AI, pseudolaw has the potential to become an existential threat: the widespread utilisation of technologies to create outputs that look legally meaningful yet are substantively nonsensical would risk fundamentally disrupting almost all aspects of legal processes. — AU; rests on abstract considerations. [CLM-0006-020]
